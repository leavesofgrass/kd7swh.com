Two Rulers: SAR and MPE

E0A08

The FCC measures RF exposure two ways, and knowing which applies where is half the subject.

SAR — Specific Absorption Rate watts per kilogram

The rate at which RF energy is absorbed by the body — the fundamental limit from 100 kHz to 6 GHz. It's how portable devices (used within 20 cm of the body — your HT against your face) must be evaluated.

  • General population: 1.6 W/kg in any 1 gram of tissue; 0.08 W/kg whole-body
  • Occupational: 8 W/kg per gram; 0.4 W/kg whole-body

MPE — Maximum Permissible Exposure mW/cm²

Power density crossing space where a person could be — derived from the whole-body SAR limit, and the practical yardstick for fixed stations like your antennas. From 300 kHz to 6 GHz the rules let MPE stand in for SAR everywhere except portable devices.

  • Your base station and antennas → evaluated by MPE
  • Your handheld → evaluated by SAR (the manufacturer's job)

Straight from the pool

E0A08 What does SAR measure?

Show the answer
The rate at which RF energy is absorbed by the body. The fake acronym expansions ("signal attenuation ratio," "signal amplification rating") are the decoys.

E0A09 Which equipment is exempt from RF exposure evaluations?

Show the answer
Hand-held transceivers sold before May 3, 2021 — the effective date of the rewritten rules. Everything since owes an evaluation.

The MPE Limits — and Why They Dip

E0A03 · E0A02

Enter a frequency, or tap a band. The limits come from 47 CFR §1.1310, Table 1 — and they are most restrictive from 30 to 300 MHz, because a human body is roughly a quarter-wave antenna there. At 6 m and 2 m, you resonate.

The chart plots both limits against frequency on log scales: high plateaus at HF, a hard floor across 30–300 MHz (controlled 1.0, uncontrolled 0.2 mW/cm²), then a climb through UHF to the microwave plateaus (5 and 1 mW/cm²). The uncontrolled limit is five times stricter everywhere above 30 MHz — and your neighbors are always "uncontrolled."

Straight from the pool

E0A03 Over what range of frequencies are the FCC human body RF exposure limits most restrictive?

Show the answer
30 – 300 MHz — body resonance. The tempting wrong answer is 300–3000 MHz. (The Technician pool's version of this idea answers "50 MHz.")

E0A02 When evaluating RF exposure at a neighbor's home, what must you do?

Show the answer
Ensure your signals are below the uncontrolled MPE limits. Neighbors can't control their exposure, so they always get the stricter tier and the longer 30-minute averaging window.

E0A06 Why are there separate E-field and H-field limits below 300 MHz?

Show the answer
All the choices are correct: the body reacts to both fields, reflections make field strength vary by location, and E and H peaks occur in different places. In the near field the two fields decouple — which is also why this Lab's far-field math is only an estimate close to the antenna.

Traps

  • Below 30 MHz the power-density numbers are "plane-wave equivalents" — the E-field and H-field limits are what actually govern. Fine for estimates; know the asterisk exists.
  • Averaging windows pair with the tiers: 6 minutes controlled, 30 minutes uncontrolled. Swapping them is the classic error.

Evaluate Your Station

E0A10 · OET-65

The real thing, per OET Bulletin 65: average power through the duty factor and the transmit pattern, spread it over distance, compare against both tiers. Preloaded with OET-65's own worked example — 1,500 W PEP SSB, 2 minutes on, 2 off — which the Bulletin says averages to 200 W. Watch the calculator agree.

S = Pavg G × refl / (4πR²)   ·   Pavg = PEP × duty × (worst-case on-time ÷ window) Ground reflection: ×1 free space · ×2.56 EPA "realistic" · ×4 worst-case 100% reflection. Far-field estimate — conservative close in.

Straight from the pool

E0A10 When must an RF exposure evaluation be performed on an amateur station operating on 80 meters?

Show the answer
An evaluation must always be performed. Since May 3, 2021 there is no categorical amateur exemption — every station either shows it qualifies for an exemption or runs this evaluation.

E0A04 With multiple transmitters at one site, who is responsible for mitigating over-exposure?

Show the answer
Every transmitter producing 5 percent or more of its MPE limit in areas where the total is exceeded. The 5% apportionment number is the thing to remember.

E0A05 What hazard is created by operating at microwave frequencies?

Show the answer
High-gain antennas can produce high exposure levels. It's the gain concentrating the power, not anything ionizing — RF, microwaves included, is non-ionizing. That decoy is always offered.

Time Averaging — the Sliding Window

OET-65 Supp. B

Exposure limits are averages: any 6-minute window (controlled) or any 30-minute window (uncontrolled) must stay under the limit. Short transmissions buy you real headroom — and the two windows can give the same station two different average powers. These are OET-65's own examples; the calculator above reproduces each one.

1,500 W PEP SSB (no processing), 2 min on / 2 min off:

Show the work
Worst 6-minute window holds 4 minutes of transmit: 1500 × 0.2 × (4/6) = 200 W average (controlled). Over 30 minutes: 1500 × 0.2 × (20/30) = 200 W too — this pattern averages the same in both windows.

500 W CW in a DX pileup, 15 seconds every 2 minutes:

Show the work
500 × 0.4 × 0.125 = 25 W average in both tiers. A big signal calling briefly is a small exposure.

250 W FM, 5 min on / 5 min off:

Show the work
Worst 6-minute window: 5 minutes on → 250 × 1.0 × (5/6) = 208 W controlled. Over 30 minutes it settles to half: 250 × 0.5 = 125 W uncontrolled. Same station, two answers — that's the sliding window at work.

Worth internalizing (asked on General/Tech, assumed at Extra)

  • Duty factor and on/off time are separate multipliers — the mode's average-to-peak ratio, then the fraction of the window on the air.
  • FM and digital modes are 100% duty while transmitting; unprocessed SSB is 20%; CW is 40%.

The Exemption Check

§1.1307(b)(3)

Before running a full evaluation, the rules offer exemptions from routine evaluation. The one hams use: ERP at or below a threshold that depends on distance and frequency. (The threshold quietly bakes in the uncontrolled limit plus worst-case ground reflection.)

Thresholds (ERP in W, R in meters, f in MHz): 0.3–1.34: 1920R² · 1.34–30: 3450R²/f² · 30–300: 3.83R² · 300–1500: 0.0128R²f · above: 19.2R² Valid when R ≥ λ/2π and R ≥ 40 cm... short version: separation matters, and the exemption is per-source. When in doubt, evaluate — it's what the calculator above is for.

The Rest of E0A

1 exam question

E0 puts exactly one of twelve questions on your exam. Eight are RF exposure (covered above); the other four are grounding and tower safety — quick wins:

E0A01 Primary function of an external earth connection or ground rod?

Show the answer
Lightning charge dissipation. Not RF-current reduction, not surge protection of equipment — lightning.

E0A07 What is meant by "100% tie-off" in tower safety?

Show the answer
At least one lanyard attached to the tower at all times.

E0A11 To what should lanyards be attached while climbing?

Show the answer
Tower legs — not the rungs, which may not be rated as anchor points. Rungs are the trap.

E0A12 Where should a shock-absorbing lanyard attach when working above ground?

Show the answer
Above the climber's head level — the shorter the free fall, the less energy to absorb.
